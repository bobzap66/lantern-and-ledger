White Lantern

A Lantern and Ledger correspondent’s guide to Embeth Hall’s best-known inn, supplemented by the municipal gazetteer and the travel recollections of Velka of the Sootscale

A lantern does not stop the storm. It tells you that someone has kept a place for you inside.

— Marna Drell

The White Lantern is a three-story luxury inn and tavern overlooking the central plaza and monument square of Embeth Hall. Known for polished floors, fine drink, attentive service, and a balcony with one of the finest views in northern Thumping Waters, it welcomes prosperous merchants without forgetting the rain-soaked travelers upon whom frontier hospitality was built.

Its white-painted lantern is visible from the northern road and, on clear nights, from the small eastern pier. For visitors arriving from Olegton, Fort Tuskwater, or the southern frontier, that light has become an unofficial sign that the dangerous portion of the journey is over.

At a Glance

Type: Luxury inn, tavern, and public house

Location: Central plaza, Embeth Hall

Proprietor: Marna Drell

Stories: Three

Known For: Lanternlight Gin, Hanspur’s Glow, merchant hospitality, music, and a balcony overlooking monument square

Clientele: Merchants, officials, craftspeople, river workers, pilgrims, and travelers

Associated Person: Velka of the Sootscale

House Saying: There is always one light left burning

A House beside the Square

The White Lantern stands near the original great hall and its adjoining festival hall, where Embeth Hall’s paved avenues meet at the central plaza. Its upper balconies face the pale stone monument to those lost in the Bloom. The ever-burning memorial lanterns remain visible from several guest rooms, an arrangement that gives the finest accommodations in town a view that is beautiful, solemn, and difficult to ignore.

Marna Drell maintains that this is appropriate. Embeth Hall was not made prosperous by forgetting its suffering. It was made prosperous by building carefully afterward. Guests who ask why a luxury inn faces a memorial rather than a garden are told that the square contains both grief and dancing, and a proper house should have windows toward the whole of life.

The inn benefits from its position at the meeting point of the trade roads from Olegton and Fort Tuskwater. Merchants use its common room to compare prices and road conditions before entering the nearby marketplace. Officials and contractors meet there after Embeth Hall’s open weekly council. Pilgrims visiting the monument or sacred grove often take quieter tables near the eastern windows.

History

Embeth Hall was founded on 18 Rova 4711 AR and grew from a small frontier village into a secure administrative and economic center. As its muddy lanes became paved streets, its palisade became reinforced walls, and its workshops began supplying finished goods across the Republic, the settlement required lodging suited to the merchants and civic visitors drawn by that success.

The White Lantern began as a well-kept public house with rooms above it. Marna expanded the building in stages, adding a third story, private meeting rooms, improved kitchens, and the balcony facing monument square. Local stonemasons provided its hearths and foundations; town carpenters built its broad staircases and gallery rails. The result is refined without appearing imported wholesale from a distant city. Its comfort is recognizably Embeth Hall’s: orderly, durable, and proudly made by local hands.

During hard winters and emergencies, the inn has served as more than a commercial house. Its kitchens have prepared communal meals, its cellar has stored civic provisions, and its common room has received displaced families and exhausted watch patrols. Marna considers these uses obligations rather than acts of charity worthy of a commemorative plaque.

The Building

The Lantern Room

The ground-floor common room takes its name from the many white-glass lanterns suspended between dark timber beams. The largest hangs above a broad central hearth. Its flame is kept burning throughout the night, even after the doors are barred at curfew, so late patrols and delayed travelers can see that the inn remains occupied.

Polished floors, stone hearths, clean tables, and attentive servers justify the White Lantern’s reputation as a luxury tavern. Its luxury is expressed more through reliability than ostentation: dry boots by morning, hot water when promised, unwatered drink, well-maintained locks, and staff who remember whether a returning guest prefers conversation or silence.

Music and laughter commonly spill into the plaza during the evening, particularly after market days and public meetings. Staff close the street-facing shutters at curfew, though celebrations connected to the festival hall sometimes continue indoors with the watch’s patient consent.

The Merchant’s Gallery

A gallery along the second floor contains small rooms that may be hired for meals, negotiations, account review, and private correspondence. Each has a writing table, lockable cabinet, good lamps, and a bell cord for service. Contracts signed in these rooms carry no special legal authority, but their occupants often behave as though polished wood improves judgment.

A public map outside the gallery records the roads to Olegton and Fort Tuskwater, the river route, known tolls, and recent reports concerning bridges or hazards. Marna does not guarantee every traveler’s information, but she records who supplied it and when.

Guest Floors

The upper stories contain private rooms ranging from compact chambers for commercial travelers to spacious balcony suites. Beds are built locally, linens are changed with conspicuous regularity, and every room has a washstand supplied with clean water. Better rooms include small fireplaces faced in gray-green Kamelands stone.

The most requested rooms overlook the monument square. They are also the quietest during ordinary nights and the least quiet during midsummer markets, remembrance observances, and celebrations in the festival hall. Marna explains both facts before accepting payment.

The Lantern Balcony

The second-story balcony faces the town hall, festival hall, and monument. It is a favored place for evening meals, political conversations conducted after public debate has ended, and the first toast of visiting trade delegations. On autumn remembrance night, the balcony is left largely unserved while the bells toll once for every name upon the monument.

When the ceremony ends, Marna offers warm food to participants and places a single cup of clear water on the balcony rail. Velka began the latter custom. No formal explanation accompanies it.

Kitchen, Cellar, and Yard

The rear kitchens are known for roasted river fish, dark bread, spiced roots, fried pike sandwiches, preserves, and meals designed to restore travelers without incapacitating them. A service yard receives goods from the marketplace and wagons from the trade road. The cellar stores wine, ale, gin, preserved food, lamp oil, and emergency water in clearly marked sections—a degree of organization widely attributed to Governor Jamel Visser’s influence upon the town.

Rainwater drains from the roof into covered storage barrels before overflow enters Embeth Hall’s municipal drains. Kitchen grease and spirits are never emptied into the street or river. Marna enforces these rules with greater severity than she applies to unpaid bar tabs.

Marna Drell

Marna Drell is the proprietor and unmistakable center of the White Lantern. She is warm without being easily deceived, generous without allowing generosity to become an excuse for exploitation, and capable of remembering a guest’s preferred drink long after forgetting the guest’s claimed title.

She treats hospitality as a disciplined craft. Rooms are inspected, cellar figures reviewed, staff paid on time, and travelers quietly assessed for exhaustion, illness, or danger. A person in genuine need is likely to receive food and a place near the kitchen fire. A prosperous visitor attempting to imitate need is likely to receive a detailed invoice.

Marna attends Embeth Hall’s open council when licensing, market rules, public safety, or the treatment of travelers is under discussion. She is neither a formal councilor nor an unquestioning admirer of government. Governor Visser values her reports because the inn often hears news from the roads before it reaches the town hall.

Food and Drink

Lanternlight Gin

Lanternlight Gin is the White Lantern’s best-known spirit: clear, aromatic, and traditionally served in a small glass beside a lantern so that the flame catches along its rim. Marna protects the exact botanical mixture, though juniper, citrus peel, and herbs from the surrounding region are readily apparent.

The gin is used in several house drinks and is sold by the sealed bottle to travelers. Each bottle bears a white lantern label and a warning that lamp oil is less expensive and significantly worse in a cup.

Hanspur’s Glow

Hanspur’s Glow combines Lanternlight Gin with a blue liqueur that shimmers faintly like moonlight upon moving water. Marna created it after Velka of the Sootscale began frequenting the inn. The drink is served in a curved glass over a single piece of clear ice, with a twist of citrus placed like a narrow crescent along the rim.

Velka insists that the shimmer represents water’s refusal to remain still. Marna says it sells better than a drink called Blue Gin with Something Floating in It. Both accounts may be true.

The Pike at the Lantern

The fried pike sandwich that Marna gave Velka on their first meeting has become an informal house specialty. It is served on dark bread with sharp greens and a pale herb sauce. Regulars call it the Pike at the Lantern; the written menu uses the more dignified River Pike Supper.

Velka and the White Lantern

Velka first reached the White Lantern drenched by a storm after following its glow through the rain. Marna gave her a towel, Lanternlight Gin, and a fried pike sandwich before asking her name or business. Velka later described that simple welcome as an act of mercy more persuasive than many sermons.

The friendship that followed has made the White Lantern one of Velka’s regular stopping places. She brings Marna river stories and pieces of driftwood, some of which are burned in the hearth for luck and others carved into small charms. Marna calls her my favorite flood hazard. Velka calls Marna a light that even the river envies.

When Velka is present, she blesses dockworkers, fishermen, and boat crews before their evening rounds. She also listens to travelers who would never deliberately seek a priest. These conversations usually occur over food, with no altar other than the table and no expectation that those receiving comfort adopt her faith.

Her Hanspurite practices coexist peaceably with the sacred grove and Embeth Hall’s other religious customs. The inn is not a temple of Hanspur, and Marna does not permit any faith to claim the common room. It is instead one of the many places where Velka treats movement, mercy, and freely offered hospitality as sacred acts.

Customs of the House

The Last Lantern

One lantern remains lit in the street-facing window until dawn. Originally a practical guide for travelers arriving after dark, it has become a promise that night workers, watch patrols, and people in immediate distress may knock even after the public room has closed.

The Road Cup

Guests departing upon a dangerous journey may request a road cup: water with a small measure of gin, a strip of citrus peel, or neither, according to preference and faith. The drink is taken standing. The traveler names the road, and the server answers, May it bring you somewhere with a light.

Remembrance Night

During Embeth Hall’s autumn remembrance, the White Lantern dims its common room while the bells sound for the victims of the Bloom. The inn displays no entertainment until the final bell. Afterward, the doors open for a shared meal at modest cost, with free places reserved for survivors and families of those named upon the monument.

Lantern Market

At the midsummer market, the inn strings white and blue lanterns across its frontage and balcony. Musicians rotate between the festival hall, plaza, and common room. Hanspur’s Glow is served in large quantities, prompting the annual reminder that resemblance to water does not make a beverage suitable for extinguishing fires.

Place in Embeth Hall

The White Lantern reflects the town around it. Like Embeth Hall, it combines frontier memory with deliberate refinement. Its stonework, carpentry, drainage, record keeping, and service demonstrate the local conviction that work done well is a civic virtue. Its crowded common room demonstrates that discipline need not exclude warmth.

The inn supports the marketplace by housing buyers and sellers, employs local cooks and servers, purchases from nearby farms and workshops, and serves travelers using the northern roads and eastern pier. Proposed expansion of the river landing would increase its trade considerably. Marna supports the project, provided the council also funds drainage, night patrols, and enough dock facilities that the inn’s entry hall does not become an unofficial warehouse.

More quietly, the White Lantern helps Embeth Hall hold together memory and hope. Its balconies overlook the lanterns of the Bloom monument; its doors face a plaza built for government, trade, ceremony, and celebration. It is a house where grief can be given a quiet table and joy can be told to lower its voice only after curfew.

People say this is a luxury inn. Fine. The greatest luxury on the frontier is knowing the roof will hold, the water is clean, and no one will ask you to earn your supper before you stop shaking.

— Marna Drell
