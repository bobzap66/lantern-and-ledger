Thumpington

Compiled by the College of Letters and Public Record, Free University of Thumping Waters, with archival assistance from the Ministry of Communication and selections from the journals of Linzi

Thumpington was not built because this was an easy place to live. It was built because enough people decided difficulty was a poor reason to leave.

— Linzi

Thumpington is the capital of the Republic of Thumping Waters, the seat of its Ruling Council, and the largest settlement on the Shrike River. What began as a rough camp of prospectors, laborers, merchants, and hopeful settlers drawn to the Crack Rock gold strike became, within six turbulent years, the administrative, commercial, educational, and cultural center of a republic stretching across much of the Stolen Lands.

The city is young enough that many residents remember when its streets were mud and its government met wherever enough chairs could be gathered. It is also old enough to have rebuilt itself more than once. Thumpington has survived frontier isolation, political unrest, the Beast of Thumpington, repeated supernatural crises, and the War of the River Kings. The result is a capital that takes pride not in being finished, but in having learned how to continue.

The City upon the Shrike

Thumpington stands primarily upon the northern bank of the Shrike River. Its oldest commercial streets descend toward Shrike Landing, where prospectors and supply boats first came ashore, while newer avenues climb toward the civic heights occupied by Castle Thumpington, Charter Hall, the ministry quarter, and residences built as the government expanded.

Endurance Bridge carries travelers, carts, and livestock across the river. The broad stone span replaced the wooden bridge destroyed during the Beast’s attack. Its keystone bears the word Endure, the same word carved upon the memorial to those killed in that disaster. Locals sometimes call it the Enduring Span, though official maps retain the formal name.

Stone walls now protect the capital. Within them, paved streets, sewers, public wells, drainage works, and magical streetlamps distinguish the city from the mining camp it once was. The lamps cast warm amber light after sunset and have become one of Thumpington’s most recognizable sights. They are admired by visitors, appreciated by night workers, and blamed for insects by citizens who believe every public improvement should also repeal summer.

The city has never followed a perfect plan. Workshops stand beside houses older than the roads serving them. Taverns occupy former warehouses. Ministries have expanded into neighboring properties when their original rooms became too small. Frontier timber buildings, rebuilt stone quarters, civic halls, temples, schools, markets, and ambitious new construction therefore exist side by side, making Thumpington’s history visible in its streets.

From Gold Camp to Capital

The Crack Rock gold strike drew the first large wave of permanent settlers. Prospectors arrived seeking wealth, followed by traders, laborers, craftspeople, farmers, brewers, guards, and families whose livelihoods depended less upon finding gold than upon serving everyone who hoped to.

On 1 Desnus, 4710 AR, the Republic of Thumping Waters was formally founded with Thumpington as its capital. The original chartered party possessed an exploration charter, several hard-won alliances, and considerably less administrative experience than the work ahead required. Early government shared space with ordinary town life. Council business, contracts, petitions, military planning, and arguments about roads took place wherever a table and enough chairs could be found.

Castle Thumpington and Charter Hall eventually established a permanent civic center. Ministry offices, official residences, the Blue Star Exchange, Hall of Common Works, embassy, Bridgeward Barracks, Goldbank Market, and other public institutions followed. Thumpington ceased to be merely the Tubthumpers’ headquarters and became a capital capable of continuing while its most famous citizens were elsewhere fighting trolls, liches, barbarians, kings, or things for which the government had not yet invented a useful category.

Government and the Ruling Council

Thumpington is the seat of the Ruling Council, which brings the Prime Minister together with seven ministers responsible for Communication, Defense, Intelligence, Justice, Magic, the Interior, and the Treasury. The council meets in the capital, while the ministries operate through clerks, officers, specialists, archives, storehouses, inspectors, couriers, and public counters spread across the civic quarter.

The current council reflects how thoroughly the Republic has outgrown government by its original adventuring company alone:

  • Prime Minister: Ally Grainger
  • Minister of Communication: Linzi
  • Minister of Defense: Ekundayo
  • Minister of Intelligence: Sean Charleton O’Finnigan McGee, better known as Seamus
  • Minister of Justice: Matteo Auditore
  • Minister of Magic: Tristian
  • Minister of the Interior: Antson “Ant” Crazyfoot
  • Minister of the Treasury: Jubilost Narthropple

Castle Thumpington contains the council chamber and executive offices. Charter Hall houses courts, civic records, petition rooms, and much of the daily administration citizens encounter directly. The surrounding ministry quarter receives reports and petitions from across the Republic, issues public notices and military orders, manages budgets and roads, oversees investigations, and produces a remarkable volume of correspondence for a government whose founders once solved most problems by walking toward them with weapons.

Thumpington’s government is one of the city’s largest employers, but national administration does not exist apart from ordinary civic life. Printers produce forms and newspapers. Merchants supply public projects and then petition the Treasury concerning payment. University scholars advise ministries while disputing their conclusions. Citizens meet the Republic through clerks, judges, guards, healers, inspectors, messengers, and queues in buildings never designed for queues.

The Beast of Thumpington

In late 4711 AR, while the Tubthumpers were away from the capital, an enormous maddened owlbear attacked Thumpington. The creature tore through streets and buildings, destroyed the wooden bridge, flattened the city’s Sarenite shrine, and killed more than thirty people before disappearing into the western woods. Gershwin the smith was among the dead. Fires and structural collapse left much of the settlement damaged, while the absence of its famous protectors caused grief to harden into anger.

The Tubthumpers returned to smoke visible long before they reached the city. After helping stabilize relief efforts, they followed the Beast into the Narlmarches and killed it on 12 Neth.

Within its lair they found a starving owlbear cub. Mairi refused to kill it for the destruction caused by its mother. She fed it, brought it back to Thumpington, and named it Lorcan, meaning little fierce one. Lorcan later became a familiar guardian around the capital’s training grounds. Citizens remain advised that a history of good behavior does not make an owlbear suitable for unsupervised petting.

Reconstruction after the attack reshaped the city. Endurance Bridge rose in stone. Roads widened. Homes were rebuilt. Sewers and drainage improved. The Sarenite community expanded its relief work through the Temple of First Light, and Tristian’s Dawn became a permanent home and school for children whose families or security had been lost.

Every Fireday, candles are lit at the memorial bearing the names of those killed. Their reflections upon the Shrike remain one of the capital’s quietest public observances.

Learning, Archives, and Public Knowledge

Thumpington’s growth has made knowledge a public institution rather than merely a possession of temples, private scholars, or adventurers returning from ruins.

The Lantern Archive, the capital’s combined public library and museum, preserves books, maps, civic records, relics, and objects gathered during the Republic’s early expeditions. Its principal research collection, the Open Treasury, now serves both the public and the Free University. The Archive’s name refers both to Thumpington’s streetlamps and to the belief that knowledge gathered at public cost should illuminate public life.

The Compass Tower served as a center of magical study before the capital possessed a university. Its collections and the records of the Ministry of Magic preserve information concerning curses, Blooms, artifacts, planar disturbances, dangerous creatures, and other problems whose solution sometimes requires more than hitting the problem again.

The Free University of Thumping Waters

The Free University of Thumping Waters was chartered in 4716 AR as a public institution independent of any single ministry, temple, patron, ancestry, or magical tradition. Its purpose is explicitly national: preserve knowledge acquired during the Republic’s turbulent first years, train people capable of serving its growing communities, and make advanced education available to people who would rarely receive it from older academies.

Its colleges draw upon the capital’s archives, workshops, roads, ministries, hospitals, and printing network. Nok-Nok College and the Nok-Nok Irregulars Fellowship deliberately recognize practical ability, unconventional experience, and community nomination alongside traditional examinations. The university’s presence has already changed nearby streets as merchants become landlords, taverns advertise debates, and booksellers discover the commercial implications of instructors disagreeing over required editions.

Printing, News, and the Public Record

The Government Printing Office grew from press components secretly purchased by Linzi and recovered from the Jaggedbriar Coven in 4711 AR. With Eobald’s help, the machinery became operational after extensive repair and assembly.

The office now belongs to the Ministry of Communication and prints laws, proclamations, forms, public notices, maps, emergency broadsheets, university materials, and other documents required by a government spread across a wide territory. It also prints the Lantern and Ledger, whose first Thumping Waters issue appeared on 1 Sarenith, 4714 AR.

Regular printing changed civic life as much as any bridge. Council decisions, casualty lists, advertisements, corrections, obituaries, settlement news, and public arguments could circulate in consistent form rather than depending entirely upon handwritten copies and tavern retellings. The Republic acquired not merely a press, but a durable public memory.

Faith, Healing, and Care

Sarenrae’s faith is especially visible in Thumpington, though the Republic has no official religion. The Temple of First Light, Sanctuary of the Dawnflower serves as the capital’s principal Sarenite temple and as a healing sanctuary, relief center, school, public kitchen, and place of counsel. Tristian helped establish the temple, but its daily life now rests upon a wider clergy led by First Dawnpriest Samira Veyd.

Tristian’s Dawn, usually called the Dawn, grew from the reconstruction after the Beast’s attack. Supported initially by Tristian, it provides long-term housing, education, meals, healing, and family placement for children affected by violence, disaster, poverty, or displacement. Its continued work reflects one of the capital’s most important lessons: emergency relief matters most when it becomes something people can depend upon after the emergency stops being news.

Second Dawn Hospital, completed during the War of the River Kings, expanded organized medical care beyond individual temples, healers, and military surgeons. The institution’s name reflects both Sarenite tradition and the hope that recovery grants each patient another beginning.

Behind First Light rises the unfinished Cathedral of the Dawn, intended for completion near the Republic’s tenth anniversary in 4720 AR. Designed to rise higher than Castle Thumpington, it is admired as a statement of faith, debated as a statement of civic priorities, and monitored by Treasury officials who insist that both interpretations require cost estimates.

Other faiths maintain shrines, priests, congregations, and visiting representatives throughout the capital. Thumpington’s religious life is as mixed as the Republic itself: inherited traditions, immigrant devotions, frontier necessity, private conversion, and citizens who become noticeably more reverent when supernatural danger begins asking questions.

Trade, Industry, and the Waterfront

Thumpington’s prosperity no longer depends principally upon gold. Goldbank Market receives grain from Olegton, metalwork from Sootscale Valley, timber from Embeth Hall, goods moving through Varnhold and the western marches, and foreign wares arriving from Brevoy and the river trade.

Shrike Landing remains crowded with cargo, passengers, messengers, and boatmen. Warehouses and workshops cluster near the piers, while mills, breweries, smithies, magical suppliers, artisans, and merchants occupy the streets rising toward the civic center. Government, construction, education, printing, hospitality, banking, transport, and public contracts now employ far more residents than the original gold camp ever could.

The Hall of Common Works mediates among established trades and newer professions created by the capital’s growth. The Blue Star Exchange, Thumpington’s principal bank and counting house, works closely with the Treasury and private merchants to finance trade and public contracts. The city has learned that national ambition creates reliable demand for stone, glass, paper, ink, lumber, food, trained labor, and explanations for why delivery will occur next week rather than this one.

Culture and Daily Life

Thumpington retains the openness and noise of a frontier town despite its ministries, university halls, monuments, and increasingly ambitious skyline. Laborers, soldiers, civil servants, scholars, priests, merchants, adventurers, refugees, and foreign visitors share its streets. News travels quickly, opinion faster, and the return of the Tubthumpers can still draw a crowd before anyone knows whether the appropriate response is applause or evacuation.

Riverlight Hall hosts music, debates, meetings, public ceremonies, and seasonal celebrations. The Dawn’s Rest caters to diplomats and prosperous travelers, while The Thumper’s Drum serves soldiers, laborers, students, and anyone who considers noise evidence that a tavern is functioning correctly.

The Roc’s Omelette, founded by cook Jamery Gerbasken, grew from the famous competition surrounding a roc egg recovered from Talon Peak. The original omelette is long gone, but the establishment remains one of the capital’s fashionable dining houses and a popular site for private celebrations, civic dinners, and people determined to prove that a frontier republic can also argue seriously about sauce.

Thumpington’s culture balances memorial and celebration. Bells and candles remember the dead. Festivals reclaim streets once occupied by relief crews and soldiers. Official ceremonies rarely remain solemn for their entire duration, partly because the Republic values humor and partly because several of its most honored citizens would regard excessive dignity as a hostile act.

Riverbend Park and Greengripe Amphitheatre

Riverbend Park opened during the Victory Festival of 6 Gozran, 4715 AR. Built after the War of the River Kings, it gave the capital a public space intended for rest rather than defense, logistics, or emergency shelter. Families, veterans, students, performers, and workers now use its lawns, paths, and river overlooks.

The Greengripe Amphitheatre stands within the park and honors Nok-Nok, Governor of Greengripe, and the members of Nok-Nok’s Irregulars who died in the war. The memorial was deliberately built as a place for music, laughter, argument, university ceremonies, and public gathering rather than as a silent monument separated from ordinary life.

May every cheer raised within the Greengripe Amphitheatre remind us that the freedom to celebrate was purchased by those willing to stand in its defense.

— Memorial dedication

Its association with Nok-Nok College has encouraged an increasing number of fireworks. Municipal authorities continue to maintain that commemoration does not suspend the capital’s rules concerning explosions before breakfast.

Principal Landmarks and Institutions

Castle Thumpington: Seat of the Ruling Council and executive government.

Charter Hall and the Ministry Quarter: Courts, petitions, records, national administration, and public offices.

Endurance Bridge: Stone crossing rebuilt after the Beast’s attack and marked with the word Endure.

Temple of First Light: Principal Sarenite sanctuary and center of healing, relief, worship, and education.

Cathedral of the Dawn: Monumental Sarenite cathedral under construction for the Republic’s tenth anniversary.

Tristian’s Dawn: Children’s home and school founded during reconstruction after the Beast’s attack.

Second Dawn Hospital: Public medical institution completed during the War of the River Kings.

Free University of Thumping Waters: Public university founded in 4716 AR.

Lantern Archive: Public library, museum, archive, and home of the university’s Open Treasury.

Government Printing Office: Ministry printing house responsible for laws, notices, maps, university materials, and the Lantern and Ledger.

Compass Tower: Center for magical consultation and research predating the university.

Goldbank Market and Shrike Landing: Principal centers of trade, shipping, and public commerce.

Riverlight Hall: Venue for performances, debates, public meetings, and seasonal celebrations.

Riverbend Park and Greengripe Amphitheatre: Postwar civic park and living memorial to Nok-Nok and the Irregulars.

Hall of Common Works: Guildhall serving crafts, trades, and emerging professions.

Bridgeward Barracks: Headquarters for guards responsible for the bridge, river approaches, and adjoining streets.

Blue Star Exchange: Principal bank and counting house of the capital.

Roc’s Omelette: Fashionable restaurant descended from the city’s famous roc-egg cooking competition.

Thumpington in 4716 AR

Thumpington entered 4716 AR victorious, grieving, and busy. The War of the River Kings no longer threatens its streets, but veteran care, reconstruction, diplomatic work, and support for damaged settlements continue. Pitax is moving toward a possible accession to the Republic, a change that would expand Thumpington’s responsibilities as surely as it expands the nation’s reach.

The capital is also preparing for dangers that armies and stone walls may not answer. Reports concerning the Wild Hunt, unrest among the fey, and discoveries beneath Candlemere have drawn the attention of the Ministry of Magic, Ministry of Intelligence, temples, archives, and the Free University. Scholars research ancient warnings while builders continue work on hospitals, roads, halls, parks, and the cathedral.

That combination defines the modern city. Thumpington is proud without believing itself finished, prosperous without forgetting ruin, and increasingly learned without losing the practical suspicion that every theory should eventually survive contact with weather, budgets, and actual monsters.

At sunset, the Shrike catches the last light beneath Endurance Bridge. The Cathedral of the Dawn’s unfinished spire turns gold above the rooftops. Streetlamps, hearths, temple windows, university halls, tavern doors, and the printing office answer one by one. The city does not shine because darkness never reached it. It shines because its people keep lighting it again.

From Linzi’s Journal: The Beast and the Broken Bridge

The worst thing about heroes is that the monsters never seem to wait for them to come home.

When the Beast attacked, Thumpington burned without the Tubthumpers there to defend it. The bridge fell. The shrine was broken. People we knew became names we had to carve into stone. By the time our heroes returned, the town was ash, accusation, and the terrible silence that follows a bell nobody has the heart to ring.

They could not undo what happened. They helped us rebuild, and then they went into the woods to make certain it did not happen again. When they returned, the Beast was dead. More importantly, they returned carrying its hungry cub instead of another trophy.

The bells rang that night for the first time since the fire. I remember the sound crossing the broken river and people stepping out of their homes to hear it together. That is how I remember Thumpington: not as the place where the bridge broke, but as the place where we learned to build one that would endure.

—Linzi

Editor’s note: Linzi maintains that she did not cry during the bell-ringing. Her earliest draft has suffered water damage in a remarkably localized area.

From Linzi’s Journal: The Hatchling

Lorcan was small when Mairi found him, although small is a relative term when discussing owlbears. He was starving, frightened, and surrounded by the aftermath of a fight he could not understand. Mairi knelt in bloodstained armor and offered him food.

People said she was bringing a monster into the city that another monster had nearly destroyed. Mairi believed that strength and kindness were not opposites. Lorcan grew beneath her care, followed her through the training fields, attempted to eat several objects whose owners considered them important, and eventually became one of Thumpington’s guardians.

The Beast destroyed our city. Its child protects it. That is not irony. It is what happens when someone decides the next story does not have to end like the last one.

—Linzi

Public notice: Lorcan remains an apex predator. Permission to admire him is not permission to pet him.
