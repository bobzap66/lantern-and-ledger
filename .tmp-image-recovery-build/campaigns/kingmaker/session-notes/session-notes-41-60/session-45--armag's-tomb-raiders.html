Session 45: Armag’s Tomb Raiders

Side

:

From Linzi’s Journal

An account of events in late Calistril, 4714 AR, reconstructed from the testimony of those present

I was not present for this expedition, which is likely the only reason the story can now be told at all. When the Tubthumpers returned from Armag’s Tomb looking as though they had personally offended history, theology, and several species of insects, I did what any responsible chronicler would do: I asked questions and sharpened my pen.

What follows is the most reliable account I could assemble from overlapping testimony, shouted corrections, and one extremely long pause whenever anyone mentioned arrows.

Trials of Stone and Stubbornness

Armag’s Tomb was not built for reverence. It was built for judgment. Every corridor and chamber seemed designed to ask the same question: Do you deserve to be here? The tomb, I should note, preferred hearing the answer screamed.

The Tubthumpers first made their way past corridors carved with lethal runes and entered a domed chamber dominated by a colossal iron statue of Gorum. The statue animated and attempted to murder them, which everyone agreed was very much in keeping with the place.

The sentinel was monstrously resilient. Blades glanced from its iron body, and fire only strengthened it. That discovery produced an admirably concise revision to the battle plan: fire bad; electricity good. Lightning split its plating, persistent blows broke through its defenses, and at last the great construct collapsed in a heap of ruined iron and offended divinity.

The tomb responded to this success by becoming less reasonable.

The High Ground

In a vast cavern, skeletal Tiger Lords waited in ambush atop sheer cliffs and loosed arrows upon the company below. They possessed all the confidence of warriors who had already died once and therefore believed the matter settled.

Matteo handed Fitzroy a potion of flight, allowing him to rise alone and engage the dead on their own ledges. Seamus and Matteo climbed the cliff by less dignified means. Velka took to the air. Ally conjured a glowing staircase and walked upward as though stairs appearing over a chasm were perfectly ordinary. Antson remained below and calmly shot the skeletal archers until they ceased to be archers—or, for that matter, particularly recognizable skeletons.

Beyond them waited Zorek, the tomb’s ancient guardian. He regarded the Tubthumpers with the weary disdain of someone who had watched generations of intruders repeat the same mistakes. Through Ally, who translated his ancient tongue, Zorek informed them that they were linguistically uncivilized, spiritually unqualified, and had completed only two of the tomb’s required trials.

Low on strength and lower on patience, they withdrew. They call this tactical wisdom. Since they survived to tell me about it, I am inclined to agree.

The Pit

While searching for the missing trial, Seamus spotted a body at the bottom of a deep pit and leapt down to investigate. The corpse belonged to one of the Iron Wraiths. The pit belonged to the ants.

An army of them erupted from the stone—far too many to fight with blades and quite capable of stripping flesh from bone. They swarmed Seamus and dragged him down beneath a living carpet of chitin. He very nearly became the next corpse left to warn future visitors.

Matteo anchored an immovable rod above the pit and hung in the air, dropping alchemical bombs into the swarm with the focused indignation of a man conducting science out of spite. Fitzroy reached for his bow and discovered, at precisely the wrong moment, that he had neglected to bring arrows. This revelation did not improve morale.

Velka ended the final swarm by firing an explosive arrow nearly at her own feet. It worked perfectly, which does not make it a sensible precedent.

Everyone was bitten. Seamus was hauled out alive. No one wishes to discuss the itching.

Zorek’s Judgment

The Tubthumpers recovered, prepared themselves, and returned. Zorek barred their way once more, but this time Seamus stepped forward.

He did not rely upon ritual or flowery speech. He named Armag for what he was: a desecrator who had come to carve his own legend into sacred stone. The others supported him, but Seamus carried the argument with conviction rather than ceremony.

Zorek listened. At last he opened the way, but offered a final warning: many might enter the inner sanctum, yet only one would prove worthy to carry Ovinrbaane out of the tomb.

This warning would soon acquire an uncomfortable significance.

Armag the Twice-Born

Armag awaited them in the inner sanctum, towering over the corpses of his own companions. Whatever divine frenzy had seized him after he claimed Ovinrbaane, it had driven him to slaughter the very warriors who had followed him into the tomb. Blood ran from the warlord’s eyes and wept from the blade in his hands.

The battle divided almost at once. Some followed Armag outside the chamber and traded blows with a warlord consumed by rage. Others remained within to face skeletal champions, a cultist of Gyronna, and summoned horrors amid shadow and deathly magic.

It nearly killed them all.

They fell, rose, rallied one another, and clawed their scattered company back together. Lightning shattered bone. Blessings held failing bodies upright. Arrows found the few openings Armag left. The surviving accounts disagree on the precise final instant, but most believe it was one of Antson’s shots that finally brought the Twice-Born down.

Armag collapsed, and the blood-weeping sword fell from his grasp.

By any reasonable measure, the Tubthumpers had won.

No Mercy for the Fallen

Reason had very little to do with what followed.

Seamus looked upon the dead warlord, the murdered companions surrounding him, and the ruin Armag had inflicted upon Fort Drelev and everyone handed over to the Tiger Lords. Whatever restraint remained in him broke. He descended upon Armag’s corpse in a fury and hacked at the dead man long after there was any enemy left to punish.

There was no spell in it, nor possession, nor ancient curse speaking through his mouth. Seamus was simply, terribly angry.

His companions could only watch. Zorek had warned them that one alone might be worthy of the sword, but the blade was not yet in anyone’s hands. Even so, it was difficult to escape the feeling that the tomb was still passing judgment—and that victory had not ended its trial.

That is where this account pauses. Armag the Twice-Born is dead, but Ovinrbaane remains, as do the consequences of everything done to reach it.

History loves heroes. It also loves costs. I suspect Armag’s Tomb has not yet finished collecting either.

—Linzi, Minister of Communication
