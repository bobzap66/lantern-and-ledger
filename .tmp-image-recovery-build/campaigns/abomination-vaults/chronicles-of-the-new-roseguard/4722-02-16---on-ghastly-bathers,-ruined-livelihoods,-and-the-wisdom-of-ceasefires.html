Chronicles of the New Roseguard

On Ghastly Bathers, Ruined Livelihoods, and the Wisdom of Ceasefires

The ninth installment in Morlibint’s retrospective history of the New Roseguard.

By Morlibint, Special Contributor — Absalom Bureau

16 Calistril 4722 AR

On the record

The spoken record expressly dates the company’s return to the sixth level to Sunday, 14 Neth. The expedition followed Theron’s week of recovery and ended with the company withdrawing from the Warped Brew rather than pressing onward in its depleted condition.

Histories of tyrants commonly end a system at the moment its ruler dies: the wicked hand is removed, the chains break, and everyone beneath it steps gratefully into freedom. This arrangement is pleasing chiefly because it is simple, whereas cruelty sustained over centuries is seldom so obliging. It teaches its subjects where to sleep, what to desire, whom to fear, and how to earn their bread, until even those who hate the tyrant may depend upon the order she imposed. One week after killing Jafaki, the company returned to the sixth level of Gauntlight and discovered that liberation had left such dependencies very much alive.

The interval had restored Theron’s strength, though his new tengu body still required him to learn what feathers, a beak, and small wings contributed to the ordinary inconveniences of armor. Kiri and Comet had likewise acquired aeon stones whose slow pulse of vitality offered their altered bodies a modest measure of healing. These preparations may sound slight beside the dangers that followed, but by day’s end slight advantages were nearly all that separated several companions from another death. They descended to the old arena and turned north toward a suite Chafkhem remembered as a spa, treating his recollection with the respect due an informed witness and the caution due information five centuries old.

The first chamber retained four changing stalls, each connected by wires and pulleys to a common lever, as well as an odor of decay and unmistakable movement behind the doors. Theron, who had missed the ordinary hazards of adventuring during his tenure as a severed head, pulled the lever and opened all four stalls at once. Four ghasts rushed into the narrow room, their stench sickening nearly everyone nearby and their claws carrying a paralysis more dangerous than the original wounds. August withdrew to cleaner air and used divine aid from the doorway; Kiri identified the creatures’ defenses and cut one down; Talia fired from the entrance while Comet’s song steadied companions struggling to keep their footing and their meals; and Malaroth consumed one of his summoned thralls to wrap its borrowed substance around Kiri as protection.

Theron ignited his sword, struck the nearest ghast twice, and observed, “This feels much better than being a head on a table.” One must permit the reincarnated their standards of comparison, particularly when those standards encourage useful sword-work. The company destroyed all four creatures and found neither prisoners nor treasure in the stalls, only evidence that a place once intended for changing clothes had become a convenient set of cages. Beyond them stood disused massage tables, beneath every one of which August searched with a thoroughness his companions first mocked and then, as repetition improved the comedy, enthusiastically encouraged; every table concealed the same stone floor.

The adjoining sauna remained functional, and water cast upon its hot stones filled the circular room with steam. Malaroth found the heat agreeable, while Talia emerged with her new feline features resembling what one witness accurately, if redundantly, called “a wet cat,” and Theron attempted to restore order to his feathers. Such domestic foolishness belongs in the record. A dungeon described only through horrors becomes an abstraction, and the people who survive it cease to sound human precisely when their humanity matters most.

Evidence that survival occasionally included steam.

Beyond the sauna lay the Warped Brew, which on earlier visits had been noisy with morlocks, music, wagers, and prospective recipients of Jafaki’s improvements. Now its musicians, customers, and most of its staff were gone, leaving only Vaschari, the long-tongued urdefhan who had managed the tavern for thirty years; Lallizanx, her drider bouncer; and Murschen, a skulk pugilist whose forehead bore the Aklo mark for victor. They appeared, in the least formal surviving description, depressed and furious. After Lallizanx identified the returning adventurers, Vaschari addressed them in Sakvroth with the succinct accusation, “You’ve ruined me.”

August replied that the company’s quarrel had been with Jafaki, who preyed upon Otari and upon them, and that they would not attack the tavern’s survivors unless forced to defend themselves. Murschen answered by stepping forward and striking Theron with the speed of a practiced prizefighter. “I would have been a contender,” he declared, for he had expected Jafaki to transform him into a champion and believed the company had murdered that future. Whether she would instead have grafted additional arms onto him and named the result progress cannot be known, although Vaschari later suspected as much; the uncertainty did nothing to reduce the reality of Murschen’s grievance in his own mind.

The battle proved more dangerous than either side seems to have anticipated. Murschen battered Theron with flurries of clawed blows, Lallizanx filled the room with fire and pinned Kiri to a wall with webbing, and Vaschari struck pressure points that interrupted the body itself before resorting to her teeth at close quarters. The tavern’s constricted approaches prevented the company from coordinating its newer methods: Kiri fell and was restored, Theron spent part of the fight paralyzed, Comet collapsed, and Murschen knocked August unconscious, while Talia and Malaroth exhausted increasingly powerful remedies merely to keep their companions within reach of help. Several attacks that might have altered the contest failed, whereas the defenders seemed to find every exposed joint and unfortunate angle the room afforded.

Chroniclers of adventurers are tempted to call any battle their subjects survive a victory, but this was plainly not one. The company had not defeated the defenders, the defenders had not secured their tavern, and both parties had reached the point at which continued fighting would exchange wounded pride for corpses. Vaschari recognized the arithmetic first and called for a halt, promising that she and Lallizanx would stand down if the company did likewise because, as she put it, “This tavern ain’t worth dying for.” Malaroth translated; Talia lowered her crossbow and raised an empty hand; August recovered consciousness into the understandable confusion of an unexpected truce; and Kiri began restoring Comet.

Courage, somewhat belatedly, in stopping.

Murschen alone declined the settlement, although not by renewing the attack. He announced that he was going to Bexland and departed with the unhurried confidence of a man who considered his point already proved, and no one attempted to stop him. Only after the weapons were lowered did the remaining parties learn why they had nearly killed one another. Vaschari had come from the Darklands because establishments like the Warped Brew were scarce below, and she had managed this one not from devotion to Jafaki but because usefulness kept her from the fleshwarper’s operating table. Its suppliers, customers, bartender, and musicians had formed a small economy within Jafaki’s larger order; when the company killed Jafaki and many of her servants, that economy collapsed with her.

None of this made the Warped Brew respectable. Its menu included captive humanoids described with mercantile indifference as “provisions,” and an explanation of Vaschari’s grievance must not be mistaken for an excuse. Jafaki’s death freed some inhabitants from a fleshwarper while depriving others of the monster upon whom their livelihoods and ambitions depended. The same act may be both just and disruptive; justice is not disproved by consequences, but it is obliged to acknowledge them.

Vaschari wished to return to the Darklands, yet Urevian and his infernal forces obstructed the route below. She proposed that, if the company removed the devil, she and Lallizanx would leave; until then, mutual avoidance would serve in place of friendship. August offered a more ambitious possibility: a shop in Otari had recently lost its keeper, and Vaschari might operate it if she obeyed the town’s laws and refrained from consuming its residents. The suggestion would have provoked objections from the council, the guard, the neighbors, the customers, and—had the prospective establishment been near my own shop—from me, and Talia supplied the immediate version of these concerns.

August nevertheless argued that ancestry did not eliminate choice. Vaschari was an individual who might live differently if given a place in which different conduct was possible; if she could not abide by the law, she could not remain. His proposal was naïve, but it was also morally serious. Less than a week after agreeing to surrender Carman Rajani to a devil, August still searched for a means of judging a stranger by choices she had not yet made. Contradiction is not invariably hypocrisy; sometimes it proves that a conscience remains active after losing an argument. Vaschari declined in any event, considering herself unsuited to surface life and preferring to go home once Urevian was gone.

Before withdrawing, Theron asked whether she knew the figure haunting Doriana Menhemes. Vaschari had not seen him, but recalled a captive muttering about “the man in colored rags” and calling him the man “that takes hands”; she had purchased the victim through morlock suppliers and knew nothing further. The testimony was fragmentary, yet it connected Doriana’s pale scholar to another victim and placed his influence among the traffic of the lower Vaults. The company now possessed a description, a name, a possible means of finding him, and evidence that hands held some significance in his predations—not enough to save Doriana, but enough to continue the search.

Thus the companions left the tavern alive, badly injured, and protected by a truce none had expected when the fighting began. Urevian still waited below, Vaschari still kept captives in her kitchen, and Murschen had gone to Bexland with his ambitions and fists intact; nothing had resolved itself neatly enough to resemble an ending. Yet everyone who lowered a weapon survived the room. Adventurers are rightly praised for continuing to fight after others would flee, but on 14 Neth their more consequential courage consisted, somewhat belatedly, in stopping.
