On Infernal Etiquette, Corpse-Built Giants, and the Uses of a Silver Key

The sixth installment in Morlibint’s retrospective history of the New Roseguard.

By Morlibint, Special Contributor — Absalom Bureau

26 Abadius 4722 AR

On the record

This installment records the first portion of the company’s final expedition against Jafaki on 5 Neth. The session transcript preserves the tactical sequence with unusual clarity, including negotiations conducted under conditions considerably less comfortable than those enjoyed by a historian examining them afterward.

The long corridor described in my previous installment did not immediately kill anyone, which provisionally qualified it as one of the safer corridors in Jafaki’s domain. The distinction lasted only until the company reached the chamber beyond, where chains covered one wall, a metal table studded with nails occupied another, and a barbed devil waited between them. Invisible scouts had evidently warned it when Captain Longsaddle opened the distant doors, and the room’s arrangement communicated both occupation and hobby. August Shepard nevertheless attempted diplomacy, beginning with the observation that devils within Gauntlight were often bound by contracts and an offer to find and destroy this one’s agreement.

To a devil, destroying a contract is not liberation but an assault upon the principle around which its society, morality, and preferred method of damnation are organized. August recognized the error and amended his proposal: the company might instead recover the document so that the devil could identify a lawful means of escaping its obligations. The correction was sensible, although first impressions possess an unfair durability in infernal negotiations. The devil disclosed that a figure named Urevian wanted Jafaki dead, a desire it did not share, and explained that its own orders were to hold the chamber, kill intruders, and fill the intervals between intrusions by torturing Jafaki’s servants.

Captain Longsaddle attempted to convert this indifference into an exchange of information. The devil replied, “Very well. Why don’t you give me the fat one? I will answer three questions. One for each hundredweight the fat one weighs.” The person appraised in this manner was Malaroth, who clarified that he was not for sale—a point one might hope did not require formal statement among companions. The devil reduced its price to one morlock in exchange for a question and a half-truth, but Kiri, having spent the previous day chained in a fleshwarper’s laboratory, was particularly unpersuaded by a proposal to surrender someone else for torture. By the time discussion ended, the company had established that Urevian commanded devils somewhere below, wanted Jafaki dead, and had soldiers who knew little about her activities but would not allow the company to leave merely because both sides had discovered a shared enemy. This was more information than many successful diplomatic exchanges produce, although one ordinarily prefers not to receive the final item while invisible creatures are taking position behind one’s back.

I had advised the company against dealing with Urevian, principally through stories about infernal bargains in which the mortals did not prosper. Fiction occasionally succeeds where instruction merely invites argument, and I stand by the warning; it should not, however, be mistaken for contempt toward those who considered the possibility. Theron remained somewhere in Jafaki’s hands, Chafkhem’s deadline was approaching, and the company had already seen what delay meant for Kiri. Under those conditions, the name of an enemy who wanted Jafaki dead sounded less like temptation than a lead. Malaroth’s detection of wings moving in the corridor interrupted the question: two zebubs, minor infernal messengers and spies, had surrounded the party while their superior entertained Longsaddle’s offer. August proposed that everyone depart without further unpleasantness, but the barbed devil explained that this outcome was not among the available terms and ordered the attack.

Infernal hospitality, priced by the hundredweight.

The fight proved shorter than the negotiation. The zebubs revealed themselves as they struck, and the company destroyed both spies together with their master before withdrawing into the now-defensible chamber to tend wounds and reconsider the route ahead. Jafaki remained behind a locked laboratory door, with other creatures standing between the company and anyone likely to carry its key. August had previously favored removing those lesser dangers methodically, but each encounter consumed healing, magic, and time regardless of whether it advanced the search for Theron. He therefore recommended reaching Jafaki by the fastest available means, subject to one substantial exception: the enormous creature sleeping in the arena could not safely be left behind them.

Jafaki might release the beast during an assault, and leaving a threat of that size at one’s back is an uncomfortable method of conserving resources. Captain Longsaddle preferred first to question the inhabitants of a nearby tavern about Urevian, precisely the direction against which I had cautioned the company, though his purpose was intelligence rather than alliance. Understanding why two enemies hated one another might expose an advantage without requiring the company to adopt the purposes of either. Kiri favored dealing with the arena first, and the others accepted that sequence. A plan need not be free of objections to become the plan everyone follows.

The arena’s occupant was a shanrigal behemoth, an aberration assembled from a multitude of corpses. Parts of its mass were translucent, and its shape appeared less constructed than disputed, as though several anatomies had failed to determine which was entitled to occupy the same space. Earlier expeditions had established that it possessed limited intelligence, considerable strength, and the ability to cast binding webs. Captain Longsaddle accordingly judged its mind more vulnerable than its body: a conglomeration of many corpses seemed unlikely to possess a single, coherent will. The deduction was sound, but the company had brought a greater supply of axes, bolts, stones, and polearms than magic suited to exploiting it.

The behemoth forced its mass into the passage leading from the arena and seized Comet, dragging the skeleton toward a mouth assembled for purposes one should prefer not to reconstruct. Longsaddle repositioned the others while Malaroth and August sustained companions whose very different physiologies required different forms of healing. Weapons that pierced the creature sank into its bulk without accomplishing as much as their wielders expected, whereas blows that cut or crushed proved more effective. Establishing this distinction required the empirical method usually employed in battle: striking the subject repeatedly with several alternatives while attempting not to be eaten.

Kiri was seized next and held while her companions attacked from either side. Malaroth sent restorative magic through the melee to sustain her, Longsaddle created an opening in which she could strike despite the grasp around her, and the company gradually wore the behemoth down until Comet’s magic finished it. Death did not immediately conclude the creature’s assault. Its accumulated flesh rotted away at once, releasing a cloud of foul necromantic force over Kiri, Talia, and Captain Longsaddle. August saw Kiri within the blast and invoked Alseta’s protection, accepting part of the injury meant for her before anyone could know what the cloud would do.

Kiri’s altered physiology rendered her entirely unharmed, while August suffered in her place for no practical benefit. The episode has occasionally been retold as a joke at his expense, which is unfair both morally and analytically: an act of protection does not become foolish because the protected person proves unexpectedly immune to the danger. It becomes expensive information. For Kiri, the discovery carried a separate complication. Jafaki’s violation had given her an advantage capable of preserving her life in precisely the place where that violation occurred, but accepting the advantage required no gratitude toward its source. A stolen body remains stolen even when one of its new properties is useful.

Several anatomies, none entitled to the whole.

Nor had the arena been unobserved. Beyond a wall of translucent stone, two seugathi researchers had watched the battle; August had spoken with one during an earlier expedition, before the company’s attacks upon Jafaki made their allegiance unambiguous. Kiri called a greeting through the wall and received an answer in Sakvroth: “It is them again. Kill them.” The researchers passed through the stone with two dreshk minions, morlocks so extensively fleshwarped that arcane power coursed through metal frameworks installed in place of living bone. Jafaki had not merely altered them but converted their bodies into instruments that could neither collapse into unconsciousness nor respond to injury as living creatures ordinarily would.

This second battle threatened the company’s minds more than the first. The seugathi spread confusion around themselves and injected Captain Longsaddle with a poison that left his thoughts sluggish; had those effects taken firmer hold, companions might have attacked one another before ever reaching Jafaki’s laboratory. Kiri resisted the influence and struck one dreshk twice in rapid succession, nearly destroying it, while Talia placed a careful shot that left another enemy bleeding. Longsaddle recovered enough clarity to kill one attacker and direct the others into advantageous positions against those that remained. The researchers died in the arena from which they had expected to observe other creatures’ suffering.

Their bodies yielded two wands, research materials, writing implements, and notes describing the adventurers’ decision to awaken the shanrigal behemoth as foolish. Talia retained the papers, perhaps from scholarly interest and perhaps because few pleasures are more exact than surviving long enough to archive an enemy’s unfavorable assessment. The search also produced a silver key. Jafaki’s principal door was the only important lock the researchers were known to pass, so the key offered the direct route August had wanted without requiring Talia to defeat the mechanism while under attack.

The company had obtained the means to end its indecision without bargaining with Urevian, though not without learning why desperation had made such a bargain visible in the first place. I would still prefer that no one look toward a contract devil for assistance, but preference does not alter the choices available to people searching for a companion under a deadline. The silver key lay among their recovered equipment, small and ordinary beside the monsters they had killed to obtain it, yet more consequential than either: a locked door permits delay, argument, and alternatives, while possession of the key makes the next decision considerably harder to postpone.
