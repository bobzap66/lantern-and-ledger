Chronicles of the New Roseguard

On the Cemetery Assault and Moadenn’s Reincarnation

The twenty-seventh installment in Morlibint’s retrospective history of the New Roseguard.

By Morlibint, Special Contributor — Absalom Bureau

24 Sarenith 4722 AR

I was in Otari when Gauntlight burned again, and for this portion of the account I need not rely upon the Roseguard’s later recollections, the testimony of townsfolk, or the peculiar arithmetic by which frightening events acquire additional participants every time they are retold. I saw the dead come out of the cemetery. I saw townsfolk running from the graveyard road, and I saw Captain Longsaddle turn an overturned wagon, several lengths of timber, and whatever labor could be gathered in the next few minutes into a barricade substantial enough to keep the greater part of the assault from reaching the town proper.

There were more dead than the wreckage visible afterward suggested. Skeletons are inconvenient witnesses to their own destruction, since a successful defense reduces an attacker to material that can subsequently be swept, burned, buried, or mistaken for the remains of several others. I will therefore not provide a number I cannot support. There were enough that the militia could not simply rush the cemetery and destroy them piecemeal, enough that Longsaddle ordered the road blocked before all of the town’s defenders had assembled, and enough that the people building that obstruction were still hammering timber into place when the first skeletons reached it.

It would also be convenient to describe the defense as orderly. It was not. The barricade was constructed while the attack was already developing, with a wagon overturned across the road, boards taken from wherever they could be obtained quickly, and citizens performing tasks for which none of them had expected to be summoned that afternoon. Longsaddle directed the work from the road itself, moving militia to threatened points, shouting for tools and reinforcements, and returning frightened volunteers to useful activity before panic could accomplish what the dead had not.

My own contribution was primarily magical, which was probably to everyone’s advantage. I reinforced defenders where I could, maintained protective workings over the most exposed portions of the line, and spent a substantial portion of the engagement informing people that stabbing a skeleton through the ribs was an inefficient use of both time and a sword. Break the joints; crush the skull; remove the legs if nothing better presents itself; and never mistake the absence of blood for evidence that an injury has been decisive. These are elementary distinctions in any serious treatment of corporeal undeath, but the average Otari dockworker has had little reason to study a serious treatment of corporeal undeath.

Longsaddle required comparatively little instruction. He understood almost immediately that elegance did not matter and that the object of the defense was simply to keep the dead on the cemetery side of the wagon until enough militia arrived to destroy them. Whenever part of the line weakened, he moved toward it. After observing this several times, I began concentrating more of my own protections upon him, since predicting where Longsaddle would be during the next crisis had become largely a matter of identifying the least desirable place to stand.

I saw him injured before I understood how badly. One blow left an arm hanging with very little useful movement, and another struck his face hard enough that the bruising remained plainly visible hours later. I told him to fall back. Longsaddle looked at the militia still arriving behind us, looked at the dead pressing the barricade, and declined without wasting enough breath to make the refusal memorable, so I strengthened the ward around him instead.

This should not be mistaken for recklessness made admirable by subsequent survival. Longsaddle knew he was injured, and anyone within several yards could see that one arm was contributing very little to the defense. He also knew that the barricade had been built under his direction, that the militia arriving in pieces rather than as a formed company were looking to him for placement, and that abandoning the position before someone else could hold the defense together might have turned an improvised line into a rout. He remained at the barricade until enough defenders had assembled that his presence there was no longer indispensable.

I have since heard several improved versions of this story. In some he slew implausible numbers of skeletons personally; in others he stood alone against the entire cemetery; one particularly enthusiastic account gives him a broken sword, although the sword I saw was intact. None of these additions is necessary. Longsaddle organized a defensive position while the enemy was advancing, continued directing it after suffering injuries that would reasonably have removed him from the fight, and remained there until the town was secure. I was present, and I have no useful qualification to add to the word heroism.

Heroism required no broken sword and no improved numbers.

By the time the New Roseguard returned from the Hunting Grounds, the attack had ended and the work had become less dramatic and considerably more unpleasant. Bones were being burned near the cemetery, disturbed graves had to be filled again, and one coffin lay beside its grave with the lid split where its occupant had forced a way out. Longsaddle’s arm was in a sling and the bruise along his jaw had darkened. When asked what had happened, he described the entire affair as “nothing to worry about,” an assessment for which I am unusually well positioned to provide contrary testimony.

The town noticed the Roseguard’s return before they had gone far. People stopped to watch them pass, and someone near the graveyard was heard to say, “Good, they’re back.” It would be easy, particularly in a retrospective account, to treat that reaction as evidence that Otari expected its adventurers to solve every danger placed before it. What I remember more clearly is relief. Gauntlight had attacked the town again while the Roseguard was beneath it, and whatever successes they had achieved underground had not yet made the people above safe.

The company joined the recovery rather than immediately turning to its own business. August Shepard and Talia Nightclaw went to the cemetery, where Vandy Banderdash and several others were resetting stones and filling disturbed graves, and spent several hours helping with the work. Kiri, whose armor had been destroyed in the Hunting Grounds, remained conspicuously less protected than usual, but the immediate danger had passed and there were graves to close before nightfall.

Their principal reason for seeking Vandy concerned Moadenn, whose preserved body they had carried back from the Darklands. The Roseguard had decided after her death that primal reincarnation should not be attempted merely because it was possible; Moadenn had known them for too short a time for anyone to claim knowledge of her wishes. Vandy could not herself provide the means to question the dead in the necessary fashion, but she knew someone in Absalom who might be able to help.

That contact was Brother Halveth of the Temple of the Shining Star, who administered Sarenite relief funds from one of the temple’s charity offices. He had written to Vandy regarding a persistent haunting that his own clergy had been unwilling to resolve by simply destroying the ghost. Vandy proposed an exchange of expertise: the Roseguard knew considerably more than most people about ghosts by then, while Halveth could potentially grant them access to funerary writings unavailable in Otari.

Before leaving town, the company attended to several other matters accumulated during its descent. Talia returned The Wizard’s Door Was Locked and acquired Ashes Beneath His Wings, while Theron accepted a battered collection of expedition accounts called The Paths That Refuse to Stay Straight. August borrowed Lanterns in the Long Winter, a history of communities surviving siege, plague, famine, and severe winters through organized ritual and celebration, which was not unrelated to his continuing plans for Candlemark. Kiri selected The Quiet Between Heartbeats, partly because she had now been paralyzed twice in rapid succession and considered it prudent for Talia to carry suitable reading material in anticipation of a third occurrence.

The two cauthooj eggs recovered beside the underground lake solved another immediate problem. Worliwynn recognized them as viable and sufficiently valuable that she could arrange either their sale or an exchange for a substantial enchanted item. The company chose the latter and obtained credit toward replacement armor for Kiri, who had little interest in meeting the eastern dragon in the clothes she happened to be wearing beneath her former scale mail.

The Roseguard also called upon Wrin Sivinxi and gave her a fuller account of what they had learned beneath Gauntlight, including the Fulcrum Lenses, the factions occupying the lower levels, and those portions of their dealings with the caligni that reflected less favorably upon them. Wrin could add nothing definite concerning the lenses, but offered to consult the stars and pass anything useful to me for inclusion in my records. Readers who have wondered how portions of these Chronicles include information unavailable in my own original notes may consider that one answer among several.

The journey to Absalom occupied the next portion of the expedition, and the problem awaiting them there proved unusually well suited to Talia’s habits. Brother Halveth’s records office had been haunted for years by Joran Vallis, a former accountant responsible for the temple’s charitable funds. The ghost was not violent. He rearranged ledgers, wrote spectral figures into account books, and repeated the same discrepancy with the persistence one might expect from a man who had died in the course of an audit: two hundred gold allocated, one hundred ninety-nine accounted.

Halveth had refused to exorcise him merely for making the office unusable. Sarenrae’s teachings encouraged mercy toward a troubled soul who had served the temple faithfully in life, and the available evidence suggested that Joran was bound by unfinished work rather than malice. Talia therefore approached the haunting as an investigation rather than a battle, which in this instance was both morally preferable and considerably less destructive to the filing system.

The records revealed that one ledger from 4682 AR was missing. Archive entries showed that it had been moved during renovations decades before, but not where it had gone; object-finding magic eventually traced it to a cabinet among funerary records. When the book was returned, Joran manifested more clearly, produced a translucent abacus, and began the audit again.

This time Talia was able to follow the discrepancy to its source. Two hundred gold had indeed been allocated to relief work, and one hundred ninety-nine had been spent upon the designated supplies. The final coin appeared under a separate entry for memorial candles used in the Vigil of the Lost. Brother Halveth understood the significance immediately: the money had not been stolen but redirected for an act of compassion, and once Joran saw what had happened to it, the account was finally complete enough for him to let go.

Whether the decisive fact was the charitable purpose of the expenditure or simply the resolution of the missing coin cannot now be established. Halveth favored the former interpretation; Talia, who possesses considerable sympathy for mysteries that refuse to remain unsolved, allowed that Joran may simply have needed an answer. The distinction mattered little to the ghost, who bowed his head as Halveth prayed and faded from the records office without requiring anyone to destroy him.

The haunting ended when compassion balanced the account.

In return, Halveth granted the Roseguard after-hours access to the temple’s restricted funerary collection. There August and Talia found and copied a working that would allow the memories retained by Moadenn’s corpse to answer a small number of questions. This was not communication with her departed soul, a distinction that would shortly prove important, but it offered them something they had not possessed when she died: evidence concerning what she herself would have wanted.

Their stay in Absalom lasted roughly two days and included several less urgent but useful errands. Theron exchanged an enchanted silver hatchet for a silver longsword through a Grand Bazaar dealer whose sign advertised “Tools for Unpleasant Work,” then sold the duskwood shield recovered beneath Gauntlight to a craftsman specializing in exotic woods. Talia obtained access to the Arcanamirium’s apprentice collections after satisfying a registrar that she knew enough arcane theory not to embarrass either herself or the institution, and spent several hours adding useful formulae to her own spellbook.

August devoted part of the visit to something more personal and took the company home to meet his family. His father, Zach Kolovan, had evidently received only limited advance warning concerning the composition of his son’s adventuring company, but accepted a dhampir shoony, an undead monkey-goblin, a reincarnated tengu, and a fleshwarped catfolk with considerably less difficulty than several ostensibly more cosmopolitan people have managed. “My wife always said if someone walks in the door hungry, you feed them first and ask questions later,” he told Kiri, and proceeded accordingly.

Kolovan asked the others what sort of person his son had become on the road, although he generally phrased the questions less directly. Theron admitted that August was often the person whose judgment he trusted when his own instinct favored immediate action, while Talia acknowledged that August possessed occasional bursts of rashness but remained more level-headed than most of his companions. By the end of the meal August told his father that he believed he had found good people to travel with. I include the exchange because histories of adventuring companies too often preserve who killed what while discarding the evidence explaining why the same people continued trusting one another afterward.

August also sought instruction from the Caydenites in a ritual called the Fortifying Brew. At the Temple of the Drunken Hero he learned from Father Branik Redcask that the working depended as much upon brewing properly as upon invoking divine favor, a theological position entirely consistent with the god in question. August helped mash grain, add the appropriate herbs, observe the necessary patience, and learn the accompanying blessing before paying the requested instructional fee by buying a round for the house. He retained the rite for later use.

On the return journey, the Roseguard finally addressed the question that had motivated the trip. Their first attempt at questioning Moadenn’s corpse demonstrated the limits of the magic: when asked whether she presently wished to be reincarnated, the body could provide no meaningful answer because Moadenn’s soul was elsewhere. August corrected the question and asked instead what she had wanted at the time of her death.

Moadenn’s response was another question: “Is the sloth dead?” She meant Nhalyss of the Resin Veil, whose mental domination the Roseguard had freed her from shortly before her death. When told that Nhalyss still lived, the corpse answered, “Then I must live to get my vengeance.” Whatever reservations remained concerning the form she might take, the question of consent was settled as clearly as the available magic permitted.

The company then gathered the rare herbs and other materials required for primal reincarnation and made arrangements to perform the rite near Yldaris. This location was chosen deliberately. Theron’s own reincarnation had left him exhausted and unfamiliar with his new body, and if Moadenn returned in similarly weakened condition, the drow settlement offered safety while she recovered.

Several days of preparation followed, during which the Roseguard repaired, replaced, and improved equipment accumulated over the expedition. Kiri’s new armor was completed, weapons and protective enchantments were redistributed where they would be most useful, and August prepared the reincarnation while Talia studied enough of the rite to assist him. The surviving chronology for these days is not perfectly consistent: one record places the company back in Otari on 6 Kuthona, while the campaign calendar later shows 8 Kuthona after the subsequent preparations. I have retained both facts rather than inventing two missing days or removing three inconvenient ones.

The reincarnation itself required several hours. August led the rite with Talia and others assisting, and the performance was not entirely clean; adjustments had to be made while it was underway, but the essential working held. The body that emerged was neither Moadenn’s original one nor a restored approximation of it. Primal reincarnation returned her as a drow woman.

Moadenn’s first concern was comparatively modest. “What happened to my hair?” she asked, having awakened in a body whose appearance differed in nearly every relevant respect from the one she remembered. August explained that she had died in the Hunting Grounds, that the Roseguard possessed no means to restore her in her former body, and that they had chosen reincarnation only after obtaining evidence that she wished to return and continue her pursuit of Nhalyss.

She chose life; the rite chose its shape.

The rite left Moadenn substantially weakened, as Theron’s had before her, so she remained in Yldaris rather than immediately joining another expedition. The Roseguard promised to help equip her once she had recovered enough to pursue the vengeance for which she had explicitly chosen another life. Their own next business lay eastward in the Hunting Grounds, where the dragon demanded by the caligni still remained.
