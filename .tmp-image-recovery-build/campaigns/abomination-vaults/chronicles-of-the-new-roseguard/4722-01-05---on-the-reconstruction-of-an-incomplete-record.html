Chronicles of the New Roseguard

On the Reconstruction of an Incomplete Record

The third installment in Morlibint’s retrospective history of the New Roseguard, reconstructed from surviving journals, notes, and later testimony.

By Morlibint, Special Contributor — Absalom Bureau
5 Abadius 4722 AR

On the record

This account was assembled after the fact from Theron’s journal, August Shepard’s notes, Kiri’s surviving notebook, later testimony, and the reconstructed campaign chronology. No complete session record survives for this interval, and I have therefore resisted the particularly historianly temptation to invent session boundaries merely because blank space is aesthetically unpleasant.

There is an unfortunate interval in the history of the company later called the New Roseguard during which the events themselves are rather better preserved than the occasions on which they occurred. This is not as unusual as one might imagine. People who expect to die in a dungeon are often surprisingly diligent about recording the monster that nearly killed them and markedly less diligent about recording whether it happened on a Tuesday. The resulting account can be reconstructed with reasonable confidence in its broad sequence, but not divided honestly into individual expeditions. What follows is therefore a reconstruction of an incomplete record rather than a series of counterfeit session records.

When last I left them, they had killed the kobolds beneath Tamily Tanderveil’s fishery, survived a green dragon, and accepted Wrin Sivinxi’s request that they investigate Gauntlight. At the time, I knew Kiri and Talia personally and knew the others in the ordinary way one knows people in a town the size of Otari: by sight, reputation, customer habits, or conversations remembered with greater clarity afterward than they deserved at the time. I did not yet think of them as a historical subject. There was no New Roseguard. There were only several increasingly bruised young adventurers who had demonstrated a disturbing willingness to descend staircases after discovering what lived beneath them.

Their first approach to Gauntlight began almost immediately. They crossed Fogfen, entered the ruined keep, fought the mitflits occupying the upper ruins, and found evidence that the place was not merely an abandoned fortification but part of a much larger magical construction. They found old workrooms, a model of Otari, signs of deliberate destruction, and passages leading downward. More importantly, they began to understand that Gauntlight’s history was tied directly to the town’s own founding and that whatever had awakened there had not done so recently by the standards that matter to ghosts.

An unpleasant landmark becomes an active question.

The company did not remain in the swamp continuously. Tamily soon sent them to reclaim an abandoned fish camp she owned outside town, the first of several troubles around Otari that repeatedly drew them away from Gauntlight. The place had acquired boars, crocodiles, rats, spiders, web lurkers, an ooze, and a scalliwing named Oli, which is an impressive accumulation of residents for property described as abandoned. The company cleared what needed clearing, relocated what could reasonably be relocated, and even allowed Oli to remain. They also encountered the ghost Blue Finley, whose unfinished hopes of becoming an adventurer had kept them from passing on. By taking Finley’s sword and, more importantly, treating an unfinished life as something other than a failed one, they helped the ghost depart.

Tamily gave the reclaimed fish camp to the company. This proved much more consequential than the original arrangement probably suggested. It became their home: a place with shared rooms, Talia’s workshop, repairs that needed doing, food that needed buying, and the mundane inconveniences without which a group of adventurers remains merely a temporary association. Looking backward from the destruction of Gauntlight, it is easy to identify some grand oath or battle as the moment the company became a fellowship. I suspect the fish camp mattered at least as much. Heroism is often credited to battlefields because historians find kitchens difficult to dramatize.

Their returns to Gauntlight carried them progressively farther below the ruins. They found traces of the missing Osprey Club thieves, learned more of Volluk Azrinae, identified Belcorra Haruvex, and discovered that the lighthouse could project spectral power directly toward Otari. They freed Borbo’s soul from the objects imprisoning it, were captured by morlocks, bargained their way out, killed the drake set against them, and eventually overthrew the morlock ruler who had attempted to use them. During the same period, Gauntlight cast its beam across Otari’s graveyard and raised the dead. At that point the question of whether the ruins posed a danger to the town ceased to be theoretical.

Otari itself continued supplying other emergencies. The missing centaur courier Narala led the company through petrification, sabotage, attacks on farms, delayed deliveries, and eventually the broader conspiracy of the Leadbuster brothers. Their investigation of fires along the flume and violence around the logging camps culminated in a disaster in which most of one camp’s workers were killed. One member of the company surrendered to secure the survival of the others. I can establish that such a surrender occurred, and that the same person was later recovered, but I cannot now assign the act a name without a degree of certainty the evidence does not justify. There are several such absences in this history. They are not omissions I have corrected; they are omissions I have learned to leave alone.

The expedition next carried the company into the library beneath Gauntlight and into contact with the Cult of the Canker. There they encountered the ghostly conservator Jarelle, fragments of Otari Ilvashti’s warning, and increasingly specific evidence concerning Nhimbaloth, the Empty Death. Gauntlight, they learned, was not simply Belcorra’s fortress. It was a machine built over something older and considerably worse. The library preserved scholarship, records, and inhabitants with equal indifference, which is perhaps the most honest description of a haunted archive I have ever had occasion to write.

It was during these expeditions that Leif Darkwood died. The exact day is lost. The company opened a chamber occupied by a powerful gaoler, refused its demand that they surrender, and attempted to retreat when the battle went against them. Leif became separated and was killed. Worse, his soul did not pass on. Gauntlight caught it and consumed it as fuel. Some of the surviving recollections around his death are themselves damaged or incomplete, and I will not improve them for literary convenience. Comet joined the company afterward, entering a fellowship already carrying a loss he had not shared and would nevertheless inherit.

The ghost of Otari Ilvashti eventually gave the company the clearest explanation yet of what lay below. Belcorra had been defeated centuries earlier by the original Roseguard, but the deeper Vaults had survived, as had forces the Roseguard never understood. A Spectral Seal barred the way onward. To break it, the company had to recover the four Icons of the Roseguard: Zarmavdian’s spellbook, Vol Rajani’s cooperative blade, Aesephna Menhemes’s hunter’s brooch, and Otari’s thieves’ tools. The search for those relics entangled the adventurers with the descendants, disputes, and unfinished obligations of the town’s founders in a manner that would eventually make the later name “New Roseguard” considerably less ceremonial than Mayor Menhemes probably intended.

The four relics had not, fortunately, all been scattered into forgotten tombs. Mayor Oseph Menhemes possessed Aesephna’s hunter’s brooch, and Zarmavdian’s spellbook had found its way into my own keeping, which is the sort of detail that becomes much more impressive when recounted afterward than it was when the object was occupying shelf space. Vol Rajani’s cooperative blade presented a less respectable problem. Carman Rajani had stolen it from the Dawnflower Library, so recovering that icon required the company to deal not with an ancient dungeon mechanism but with a living descendant who had decided that an heirloom belonging to Otari’s civic history would be better employed as his private property. Otari’s thieves’ tools were more intimate still. They remained with his corpse, found only after the company freed his ghost from the wisps that had been feeding upon his despair. The adventurers were therefore obliged, in assembling the Icons, to negotiate with the living, consult the dead, recover stolen property, and in one instance ask their local bookseller whether he happened to have retained a centuries-old wizard’s spellbook. I did. Historians are occasionally useful in ways that do not involve correcting plaques.

Before the seal was opened, another Otari problem intervened. The search for a missing champion of Sarenrae took the company into the Scar and the cult of Lamashtu occupying it. They deciphered the Three-Eyed Sight and the Test of the Five Beasts, dealt with kobolds recruited by the cult, fought their way through the dungeon, defeated Mwibah, and recovered the sacred Sun Wheel. Thus the third of Tamily’s broader troubles around Otari was resolved while the much larger problem beneath Gauntlight continued waiting for them. In retrospect the interleaving seems almost absurd: civic errands, local mysteries, and errands for priests occurring beside an escalating confrontation with a centuries-dead sorcerer. At the time, I suspect it simply felt like life in Otari had become unusually busy.

When the four Icons were finally brought before the Spectral Seal, their purpose became apparent. The relics themselves called forth the spirits of the original Roseguard. Otari Ilvashti, Aesephna Menhemes, Vol Rajani, and Zarmavdian appeared together before the company that had gathered their belongings and taken up the work they had left unfinished centuries before.

Each of the old heroes approached one or two members of the company and whispered something meant only for them—advice, encouragement, warning, perhaps merely recognition. Exactly what was said is absent from the surviving record. If those words were remembered, their recipients kept them private, and I see no reason to improve history by inventing confidences they chose not to share.

Then the original Roseguard turned upon the seal. They rushed the barrier together, not with weapons so far as the witnesses could determine, but with the force of their own spirits. The Spectral Seal shattered under the impact, and the four heroes vanished with it.

One final service; no certain farewell.

What became of them afterward was never known. The company could not tell whether breaking the seal finally released the Roseguard to travel onward to the Boneyard, or whether Gauntlight claimed them as it had claimed Leif Darkwood and consumed their souls to fuel its machinery. By then the adventurers understood enough about the Vaults to know that disappearance was not proof of peace. The uncertainty remained with them: the old Roseguard had returned when their Icons called, had finished one last piece of unfinished work, and then were simply gone.

Beyond the broken seal lay Belcorra’s old arena and training grounds, and eventually the way toward the laboratories of the seugathi fleshwarper Jafaki. There the company’s history becomes both more terrible and more difficult to reconstruct. Chafkhem, an administrator trapped for centuries, offered information in exchange for proof of Jafaki’s death. The adventurers found prison cells, sealed doors, keys, captives, and evidence of the fleshwarper’s experiments. What followed was not one clean disaster but a succession of them.

Talia was the first to fall. A gibtas bounder knocked her through a hole in the floor, and she plunged roughly sixty feet into a pool on the level below. Two hydras occupied the water. Comet, Kiri, and Theron jumped after her with the sort of immediate loyalty that is admirable chiefly because there is never enough time during such decisions to calculate how catastrophically unwise they are. Another member of the company remained above long enough to finish the bounders before joining August in what came afterward; once again, the action survives more clearly than the actor.

The hydras overwhelmed the rescuers. Theron and Talia were knocked unconscious near the shore of the underground lake. Talia stabilized. Theron did not, and bled to death there. Comet and Kiri were struck down while still in the water before they could reach shore, and Comet drowned. With four companions lost below, August and the remaining member of the expedition returned to Otari to gather a rescue party.

Talia was found first on the sixth level, alive but no longer physically unchanged. Before her rescue, Jafaki’s servants had subjected her to fleshwarping, leaving her with aquatic alterations including fins. The distinction matters: she was not merely injured and healed, but forcibly transformed. That difference would become important repeatedly in the months and years afterward. Comet was found later in the Warped Brew, a tavern within the Vaults whose clientele consisted largely of aberrations and fleshwarped inhabitants. He had died, yet he was moving again as an animated skeleton and initially remembered nothing of himself. One can argue at length over the correct thaumaturgical classification of such a condition; at the time, the more urgent fact was that Comet was back and did not know who Comet was.

Recovered did not mean unchanged.

Kiri and Theron remained missing. The rescue party assembled in Otari included Captain Lardus Longsaddle; Kiri’s brother, Kai Steelpaws, who had considerably more personal reason than most to follow a trail into Jafaki’s domain; Zarah Cinderfury, a talkative kobold alchemist; and Miles, a taciturn close-quarters fighter recruited at the Crook’s Nook, whose exact martial discipline is not preserved in the surviving record. They descended into the Vaults in search of all four missing adventurers. Zarah departed after Talia was recovered, and Miles did the same once Comet had been found, leaving Longsaddle and Kai to continue the search alongside the restored members of the company while Kiri and Theron remained missing. Theron’s fate was, by then, already decided beneath the lake, though his companions had not yet reached the end of his story. Kiri’s was still unfolding somewhere ahead of them.

This is where the continuous reconstruction must stop. The next surviving direct record begins with the rescue party already caught in Jafaki’s so-called spies’ hallway, a corridor of shifting pendulum blades, still searching for Kiri and Theron. From that point forward, the record becomes substantially firmer. It was only after those events that Talia began making a deliberate effort to keep me informed of the company’s progress, which is both gratifying and, from a historian’s perspective, several weeks later than would have been convenient.

Everything before that point has therefore been assembled backward: from journals, notes, remembered conversations, later testimony, and the surviving shape of consequences. I have tried not to make the reconstruction cleaner than the events deserve. The company acquired a home, lost friends, gained another, defended Otari from threats that seemed unrelated until they were not, and descended from an old ruined lighthouse into a system designed to transform bodies, souls, and memory alike. By the time the surviving record resumes, the people who had once gone beneath Tamily’s fishery to kill rats were already nearly unrecognizable from the adventurers who had begun there.
